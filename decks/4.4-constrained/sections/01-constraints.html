Optimising on a curve

Find the largest and smallest values of \(f(x, y) = xy + 1\), but only on the unit circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\).

A saddle, seen along a circle

  • \(f = xy + 1\) is a saddle. On its own it has no highest point.
  • Lift the unit circle onto the surface: above the circle, \(f\) traces a closed loop.
  • The loop has two highs at \(1.5\) and two lows at \(0.5\).

Raise the level until it only touches

  • A level curve \(xy + 1 = c\) that crosses the circle is not the best yet: \(f\) is larger further along the circle.
  • The highest level that still meets the circle only touches it.
  • There \(\nabla f\) lies on the same line as \(\nabla g\).
Act 1 / Why the gradients line up

Touching curves share a tangent line

Write the constraint as \(g(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - 1 = 0\). The circle is the level curve \(g = 0\).

  1. A gradient is perpendicular to its own level curve. So \(\nabla g\) is perpendicular to the circle.
  2. \(\nabla f\) is perpendicular to the level curve of \(f\) through the same point.
  3. Where the two curves only touch, they share one tangent line. Both gradients are perpendicular to it.
  4. In the plane, two vectors perpendicular to the same line lie on one line: \(\nabla f = \lambda\,\nabla g\) for some number \(\lambda\).

At the highs \(\lambda = \tfrac12\) (same direction); at the lows \(\lambda = -\tfrac12\) (opposite).
