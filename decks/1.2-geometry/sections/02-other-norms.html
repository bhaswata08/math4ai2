Act 2 / Three norms

One vector, three lengths

NormRule\(\vec v = [3, 2]\)
L1 · Manhattan\(\lVert \vec v \rVert_1 = \sum_i |v_i|\)3 + 2 = 5
L2 · Euclidean\(\lVert \vec v \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\sum_i v_i^2}\)√13 ≈ 3.61
L∞ · Chebyshev\(\lVert \vec v \rVert_\infty = \max_i |v_i|\)max(3, 2) = 3

Always \(L_\infty \le L_2 \le L_1\). Which one you use is a modelling choice.

Act 2 / One family

L3, L4 and beyond: the Minkowski norms

$$\lVert \vec v \rVert_p = \big(|v_1|^p + |v_2|^p + \cdots + |v_n|^p\big)^{1/p}, \qquad p \ge 1$$
Norm\(\vec v = (10, 2)\)
L1\(10 + 2\)12
L2\(\sqrt{10^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{104}\)≈ 10.198
L3\(\sqrt[3]{10^3 + 2^3} = \sqrt[3]{1008}\)≈ 10.026
L4\(\sqrt[4]{10^4 + 2^4} = \sqrt[4]{10016}\)≈ 10.004
L∞\(\max(10, 2)\)10

Raising to a higher \(p\) makes the largest entry grow far faster than the rest, so it takes over.

Act 2 / Why the max

Why \(p \to \infty\) picks the largest entry

$$\lVert \vec x \rVert_p = \big(|x_1|^p + |x_2|^p + \cdots + |x_n|^p\big)^{1/p}$$definition; let \(|x_k|\) be the largest entry
$$= \Big(|x_k|^p \Big(\Big(\tfrac{|x_1|}{|x_k|}\Big)^p + \cdots + \Big(\tfrac{|x_k|}{|x_k|}\Big)^p + \cdots + \Big(\tfrac{|x_n|}{|x_k|}\Big)^p\Big)\Big)^{1/p}$$pull \(|x_k|^p\) out of every term
$$= |x_k| \Big(1 + \sum_{i \ne k} \Big(\tfrac{|x_i|}{|x_k|}\Big)^p\Big)^{1/p}$$the \(k\)-th term is 1, and \((|x_k|^p)^{1/p} = |x_k|\)
$$\Big(\tfrac{|x_i|}{|x_k|}\Big)^p \xrightarrow{\;p \to \infty\;} 0 \quad (i \ne k)$$each ratio is below 1, so its powers shrink to 0
$$\lim_{p \to \infty} \lVert \vec x \rVert_p = |x_k| \cdot 1 = |x_k|$$the bracket goes to 1, and so does its \(1/p\)-th power
$$\boxed{\lVert \vec x \rVert_\infty = \lim_{p \to \infty} \lVert \vec x \rVert_p = \max_i |x_i|} \quad \text{the Chebyshev norm, or chessboard distance}$$

ties for the largest entry: the bracket stays below n, and n^(1/p) → 1. AdaMax (Kingma and Ba, 2015) uses this limit: it swaps Adam's average of squared gradients for a running max of |g|.

Act 2 / Where this shows up

Penalising weights is a norm

Ridge

Loss \(+ \lambda \lVert \vec w \rVert_2^2\). Shrinks every weight a little.

Lasso

Loss \(+ \lambda \lVert \vec w \rVert_1\). Pushes many weights to exactly zero.

Weights \(\vec w = [0.5, -1, 2]\) (illustrative values):

\(\lVert \vec w \rVert_1 = 0.5 + 1 + 2 = 3.5\)

\(\lVert \vec w \rVert_2^2 = 0.25 + 1 + 4 = 5.25\)
