Unit vectors

Keep the direction, throw the length away.

Divide by your own length

Each vector lands on the unit circle, still pointing the same way. \(\hat v = \vec v / \lVert \vec v \rVert\).

Act 3 / Example

Normalizing [3, 4]

$$\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \lVert \vec v \rVert = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5, \quad \textcolor{#f0a35e}{\hat v} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \textcolor{#f0a35e}{\begin{bmatrix} 0.6 \\ 0.8 \end{bmatrix}}$$

Check: \(0.6^2 + 0.8^2 = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1\).

The zero vector has no direction, so it cannot be normalized.

Act 3 / Which length?

A unit vector always means L2 length 1

Rotation does not change it

Turn \([1, 0]\) by 45° to \([0.71, 0.71]\): L2 stays 1, but L1 becomes 1.41 and L∞ becomes 0.71. Only L2 is a property of the arrow, not of the grid.

Smooth to differentiate

\(\lVert \vec v \rVert_2^2 = \vec v \cdot \vec v\) has gradient \(2\vec v\) everywhere. L1 has a kink wherever an entry is 0, and L∞ wherever two entries tie.

Tied to angles

\(\lVert \vec v \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\vec v \cdot \vec v}\), so for unit vectors \(\hat v \cdot \hat w = \cos\theta\) with nothing left to divide.

"Normalize" means divide by the L2 norm, unless someone says otherwise.
