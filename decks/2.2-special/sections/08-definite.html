Act 8 of 10

Positive definite

Symmetric matrices that curve upward in every direction.

Water pools at the bottom of a bowl

  • How does an optimiser know it has reached a true minimum, and not a flat plateau?
  • Training looks for the lowest point of a loss: the lowest energy.
  • Water in a bowl always settles at its one lowest point.
  • The matrix behind that bowl is called positive definite.

\(x^TAx\): a vector in, one number out

  • \(x^TAx\): a vector in, one number out (an energy, or a height).
  • \(A\) stretches and turns \(x\) into \(Ax\).
  • Here \(x\) lies almost on a direction \(A\) only stretches, so \(Ax\) barely turns.
  • The dot product with \(x\) measures how much \(Ax\) still points along \(x\).
  • Positive for every \(x\neq0\): \(A\) is positive definite.
Act 8 / Quadratic form

One number from a matrix and a vector

Take a square matrix \(A\) and a vector \(x\). First \(A\) moves \(x\) to \(Ax\), then we take the dot product with \(x\) again:

$$x^TAx \;=\; x\cdot(Ax) \;=\; \sum_i\sum_j a_{ij}\,x_i\,x_j$$

Every term is two coordinates multiplied together: \(x_1^2\), \(x_1x_2\), \(x_2^2\), and so on. No constants, no linear terms. This is called a quadratic form.

The angle test

  • \(x^TAx=\|x\|\,\|Ax\|\cos\theta\).
  • The lengths are positive, so the sign comes from \(\cos\theta\).
  • Positive definite: \(Ax\) never turns more than 90° away from \(x\), whatever \(x\) is.
Act 8 / Quadratic form

The anti-symmetric part cancels out

When evaluating a quadratic form \(x^TAx\) with a general \(2\times2\) matrix:

$$\begin{bmatrix}x_1&x_2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a&p\\q&c\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}=ax_1^2+(p+q)x_1x_2+cx_2^2$$

The individual values of the off-diagonal entries \(p\) and \(q\) do not matter independently—only their sum \((p+q)\) appears in the resulting scalar expression.

Act 8 / Quadratic form

Symmetrizing changes nothing in the output

If you replace both \(p\) and \(q\) by their average:

$$b=\frac{p+q}{2}$$

the matrix becomes symmetric:

$$\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{bmatrix}$$

The resulting quadratic form is identical:

$$ax_1^2+2bx_1x_2+cx_2^2=ax_1^2+(p+q)x_1x_2+cx_2^2$$
Act 8 / Quadratic form

Generalization to any size matrix

Any square matrix \(A\) can be decomposed into a symmetric part and a skew-symmetric (anti-symmetric) part:

$$A=\underbrace{\frac{A+A^T}{2}}_{\text{symmetric}}+\underbrace{\frac{A-A^T}{2}}_{\text{skew-symmetric}}$$

For any skew-symmetric matrix \(K\) (where \(K^T=-K\)), the quadratic form is always zero:

$$x^TKx=0\quad\text{for all }x$$

Therefore:

$$x^TAx=x^T\left(\frac{A+A^T}{2}\right)x$$

Every direction gets a height

  • Sweep \(x\) round the plane. \(x^TAx\) gives a height above each point.
  • Positive definite: every height is positive.
  • All directions together form a bowl that curves up.
  • The origin is the lowest point, so water cannot escape.

Steep and gentle directions

  • The bowl has two main axes, at right angles.
  • Along each axis the cut is a parabola with steepness \(\lambda\).
  • Large \(\lambda\): steep, narrow walls. Small \(\lambda\): a gentle, wide slope.
  • Up in every direction exactly when every \(\lambda>0\). Block 2.4 calls these \(\lambda\)'s eigenvalues.
Act 8 / Curvature

Reading the curvatures off a diagonal matrix

Take \(A=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1&0\\0&\lambda_2\end{bmatrix}\). Then \(x^TAx=\lambda_1x_1^2+\lambda_2x_2^2\).

Walk along the \(x_1\) axis (\(x_2=0\)): the height is \(z=\lambda_1x_1^2\), a parabola with steepness \(\lambda_1\). Along the \(x_2\) axis: \(z=\lambda_2x_2^2\).

Both \(\lambda\) positive: up both ways, a bowl. One zero: flat along that axis, a trough. Opposite signs: a saddle. Both negative: a bowl upside down.

A symmetric \(A\) that is not diagonal gives the same picture along two tilted axes at right angles. Block 2.4 finds those axes (eigenvectors) and the \(\lambda\)'s (eigenvalues).

Definiteness is the sign pattern of the curvatures.

One curvature negative: a saddle

  • What if one \(\lambda\) is negative? The matrix is indefinite.
  • Up along one axis, down along the other: a saddle.
  • Water at the centre spills off the sides.
  • The slope at the centre is zero, so an optimiser can think it has finished.

Four landscapes from above

  • All \(\lambda>0\): positive definite, rings round one lowest point.
  • Some \(\lambda=0\), the rest positive: semi-definite, a trough with a whole line of lowest points.
  • All \(\lambda<0\): negative definite, an upside-down bowl.
  • Mixed signs: indefinite, a saddle.
  • Ask: which is hardest to navigate in thousands of dimensions?

Test the top-left corners

  • Finding the curvatures of a big matrix is expensive.
  • Sylvester's test checks the top-left corners instead.
  • For a \(2\times2\): the corner \(a>0\), then the whole matrix \(ac-b^2>0\).
  • All corners positive: positive definite. Block 2.3 calls these numbers determinants.
Act 8 / Testing

A quick test for a symmetric 2×2

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{bmatrix}\ \text{is PD}\iff a>0\ \text{and}\ ac-b^2>0$$ $$x^TAx=\begin{bmatrix}x_1&x_2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}=ax_1^2+2bx_1x_2+cx_2^2$$

Complete the square (\(a\neq0\)):

$$ax_1^2+2bx_1x_2+cx_2^2=a\Big(x_1+\tfrac{b}{a}x_2\Big)^2+\tfrac{ac-b^2}{a}\,x_2^2$$

Both squares are positive for every \(x\neq0\) exactly when \(a>0\) and \(ac-b^2>0\).

Check: \(\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\) has \(a=2>0\) and \(ac-b^2=3>0\): PD. \(\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}\) has \(ac-b^2=-1\): not PD.

The test applies to symmetric matrices only.

Worked example

  • \(A=\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&3\end{bmatrix}\)
  • Corner: \(4>0\).
  • Whole matrix: \(4\cdot3-2\cdot2=8>0\). Positive definite.
  • Curvatures about 5.56 and 1.44, both positive: a steep, tilted elliptical bowl.

The Hessian plays the role of \(A\)

  • A real loss is a curved landscape, not one fixed \(x^TAx\).
  • The Hessian \(H\) collects the second derivatives of the loss.
  • Where the gradient is zero, \(H\) plays the role of \(A\).
  • It sets the curvature in every direction around that point (Day 4).

A positive definite Hessian means a true minimum

  • \(H\) positive definite: every curvature is positive.
  • The loss curves up in every direction, like a bowl.
  • So the flat point is not a saddle or a plateau. It is a local minimum.
  • Gradient descent steps funnel down to the bottom.
Act 8 / Always PSD

\(X^TX\) is never negative

$$x^T(X^TX)x=(Xx)^T(Xx)=\|Xx\|^2\ \ge 0$$

Covariance

Built as \(X^TX\) (centred data), so it is PSD. A variance cannot be negative.

Hessian (Day 4)

At a strict minimum the Hessian is positive definite: the loss curves up in every direction.
