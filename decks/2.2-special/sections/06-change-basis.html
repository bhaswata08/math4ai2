Act 6 of 10

Changing basis

The same arrow can have different coordinates. A matrix and its inverse translate between them.

What \([3, 2]\) means

The numbers are instructions: 3 steps along \(\hat\imath\), then 2 steps along \(\hat\jmath\). Every coordinate silently assumes these two vectors.

Jennifer's basis

Jennifer uses different first steps \(\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec b_1}\) and \(\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec b_2}\), so her grid is different. We agree only on the origin.

Act 6 / Two languages

Same arrows, different numbers

we writeJennifer writes
\(\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec b_1}\)\([2, 1]\)\([1, 0]\)
\(\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec b_2}\)\([-1, 1]\)\([0, 1]\)
\(\textcolor{#f0a35e}{\vec x}\)\([3, 2]\)\([5/3,\ 1/3]\)

To Jennifer, her own basis vectors are \([1, 0]\) and \([0, 1]\), just as \(\hat\imath, \hat\jmath\) are to us.

The arrow is the same. Only the description changes. We need a way to translate.

One arrow, two descriptions

We reach \(\textcolor{#f0a35e}{\vec x}\) with \(3\hat\imath + 2\hat\jmath\). Jennifer reaches it with \(\tfrac53\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec b_1} + \tfrac13\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec b_2}\).

Her numbers to ours

Jennifer says \([-1, 2]\). That means \(-1\,\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec b_1} + 2\,\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec b_2}\). Build it, then read the tip on our grid.

Act 6 / Her numbers to ours

Scale her basis vectors, then add

We know her basis vectors in our numbers, so we can do the sum ourselves:

$$-1\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} + 2\textcolor{#c792ea}{\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} = \textcolor{#f0a35e}{\begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}$$

This is a matrix times a vector (Block 2.1): the columns of the matrix are mixed by the entries of the vector.

$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \textcolor{#6fd0c0}{2} & \textcolor{#c792ea}{-1} \\ \textcolor{#6fd0c0}{1} & \textcolor{#c792ea}{1} \end{bmatrix}}_{B}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \textcolor{#f0a35e}{\begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}$$
Act 6 / Her numbers to ours

The change-of-basis matrix

$$B = \begin{bmatrix} \textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec b_1} & \textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec b_2} \end{bmatrix}\quad\text{(her basis vectors, written in our numbers)}$$
$$B\,\vec x_{\text{hers}} = \vec x_{\text{ours}}$$

Feed \(B\) Jennifer's numbers. Out come our numbers for the same arrow.

What \(B\) does to the grid

As a transformation, \(B\) moves \(\hat\imath\) to \(\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec b_1}\) and \(\hat\jmath\) to \(\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec b_2}\): our grid lands on hers.

Act 6 / Her numbers to ours

Isn't that backwards?

\(B\) moves our grid onto hers, yet it translates her numbers into ours. Follow the clip step by step:

  1. Jennifer says \([-1, 2]\). We misread it on our grid as \(-1\,\hat\imath + 2\,\hat\jmath\): the wrong arrow.
  2. \(B\) sends \(\hat\imath \to \textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec b_1}\) and \(\hat\jmath \to \textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec b_2}\), so it carries that arrow to \(-1\,\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec b_1} + 2\,\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec b_2}\): the arrow she meant.
  3. Its tip sits at \(\textcolor{#f0a35e}{[-4, 1]}\) on our grid. Those are our numbers.

\(B\) turns our misreading of her numbers into the arrow she actually meant.

Act 6 / Our numbers to hers

The other direction: undo \(B\)

Going from ours to hers undoes \(B\), so use the inverse (Act 5):

$$\vec x_{\text{hers}} = B^{-1}\vec x_{\text{ours}},\qquad B^{-1} = \tfrac13\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$
$$B^{-1}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \tfrac13\begin{bmatrix} 3 + 2 \\ -3 + 4 \end{bmatrix} = \textcolor{#f0a35e}{\begin{bmatrix} 5/3 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix}}$$

The \([5/3,\ 1/3]\) Jennifer used for \(\vec x\) in the table.

What \(B^{-1}\) does to the grid

\(B^{-1}\) moves her grid back onto ours. Our \([3, 2]\) lands on her coordinates \([5/3,\ 1/3]\).

Act 6 / Summary

One matrix, two directions

$$\underbrace{\vec x_{\text{ours}} = B\,\vec x_{\text{hers}}}_{\text{her numbers to ours}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{\vec x_{\text{hers}} = B^{-1}\vec x_{\text{ours}}}_{\text{our numbers to hers}}$$

\(B^{-1}\) always exists: \(\vec b_1, \vec b_2\) are a basis, so they are independent and \(B\) squashes nothing.

Act 6 / Translating a transformation

A rotation, in Jennifer's numbers

We write a \(90^\circ\) turn as \(M = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\). Its columns say where \(\hat\imath\) and \(\hat\jmath\) land, in our numbers.

Jennifer cannot use \(M\). If she feeds it her \([-1, 2]\), \(M\) reads those as our numbers and turns the wrong arrow.

Her version must say where her basis vectors land, in her numbers.

Where her basis vectors land

Turn \(\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec b_1}\) and \(\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec b_2}\) by \(90^\circ\), then read the results on her grid. Those numbers are the columns of the rotation in her language.

Act 6 / Translating a transformation

Translate, rotate, translate back

Start with a vector in her numbers and read right to left:

$$B^{-1}\,M\,B\,\vec x_{\text{hers}}$$
  1. \(B\,\vec x_{\text{hers}}\): the same arrow, in our numbers
  2. \(M\): rotate it, still in our numbers
  3. \(B^{-1}\): the rotated arrow, back in her numbers
$$B^{-1}MB = \tfrac13\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 & -2/3 \\ 5/3 & -1/3 \end{bmatrix}$$

The same matrix the clip read off her grid.

Act 6 / A better basis

Orthonormal basis: \(B^{-1} = B^{T}\)

If the basis vectors \(\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1}, \textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2}\) are unit length and at right angles, \(B\) is orthogonal (Act 3), so \(B^{-1} = B^{T}\):

$$\vec x_{\text{new}} = B^{T}\vec x = \begin{bmatrix} \textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1}^{T} \\ \textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2}^{T} \end{bmatrix}\vec x = \begin{bmatrix} \vec x\cdot\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1} \\ \vec x\cdot\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2} \end{bmatrix}$$

Example: \(\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1} = [0.6, 0.8]\), \(\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2} = [-0.8, 0.6]\), \(\vec x = [3, 2]\) gives \(3(0.6) + 2(0.8) = \textcolor{#f0a35e}{3.4}\) and \(3(-0.8) + 2(0.6) = \textcolor{#f0a35e}{-1.2}\).

Each new coordinate is one dot product. No inverse to compute.
