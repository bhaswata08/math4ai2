Data in a new basis

Every new coordinate is a dot product, so one matrix product rewrites the whole dataset.

Act 2 / Setup

The data matrix

$$X = \begin{bmatrix} \text{—}\ \mathbf x_1^T\ \text{—} \\ \vdots \\ \text{—}\ \mathbf x_m^T\ \text{—} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb R^{m \times n}$$
  • \(m\) data points (rows), \(n\) features (columns).
  • Centre every column (subtract its mean), so \(\sum_i \mathbf x_i = \mathbf 0\). Assumed from here on.
  • Features in different units (cm and km)? Also divide each column by its standard deviation, or the largest numbers dominate.
  • New basis: \(P = [\,\mathbf p_1 \cdots \mathbf p_n\,]\) with orthonormal columns, so \(P^TP = I\).
Act 2 / Coordinates

The Core Idea: Coordinates via Dot Products

Suppose you have an orthonormal basis \(\{\mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_2, \dots, \mathbf{p}_n\}\). Orthonormal means:

  • Every basis vector has unit length: \(\mathbf{p}_k^T \mathbf{p}_k = 1\)
  • Every pair of distinct basis vectors is perpendicular: \(\mathbf{p}_j^T \mathbf{p}_k = 0\) for \(j \neq k\)

Any data point \(\mathbf{x}_i\) can be written as a linear combination of these basis vectors:

$$\mathbf{x}_i = \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_{ij} \mathbf{p}_j$$

Here, \(\alpha_{ik}\) represents the coordinate (weight/projection) of \(\mathbf{x}_i\) along the direction \(\mathbf{p}_k\).

Act 2 / Coordinates

The Core Idea: Coordinates via Dot Products

To find the value of \(\alpha_{ik}\), take the inner product (dot product) of both sides with \(\mathbf{p}_k\):

$$\mathbf{x}_i^T \mathbf{p}_k = \sum_{j} \alpha_{ij} (\mathbf{p}_j^T \mathbf{p}_k)$$

Because the basis is orthonormal:

  • \(\mathbf{p}_j^T \mathbf{p}_k = 0\) for all \(j \neq k\)
  • \(\mathbf{p}_j^T \mathbf{p}_k = 1\) when \(j = k\)

Every term in the summation vanishes except when \(j = k\), leaving: \(\quad\alpha_{ik} = \mathbf{x}_i^T \mathbf{p}_k\)

This proves that to find a point’s coordinate along an orthonormal axis, simply take the dot product of the point with that axis vector.

One point, then all of them

Coordinate \(\alpha_{ij}\) is the length of the shadow of \(\mathbf x_i\) on \(\mathbf p_j\). Row \(i\) of \(XP\) holds all of them.

Act 2 / Coordinates

Vectorizing for a Single Data Point

To get all \(n\) new coordinates \([\alpha_{i1}, \alpha_{i2}, \dots, \alpha_{in}]\) for a single point \(\mathbf{x}_i\):

  • Pack the basis vectors into the columns of a matrix \(P = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{p}_1 & \mathbf{p}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{p}_n \end{bmatrix}\) (size \(n \times n\)).
  • Multiply the row vector \(\mathbf{x}_i^T\) (\(1 \times n\)) by \(P\):
$$[\alpha_{i1}, \dots, \alpha_{in}] = \mathbf{x}_i^T P$$

One row times \(P\)

Entry \(k\) of \(\mathbf x_i^T P\) is the row \(\mathbf x_i^T\) against column \(\mathbf p_k\): \(\mathbf x_i^T\mathbf p_k = \alpha_{ik}\).

Stacking All \(m\) Data Points (Matrix Form)

If you have \(m\) data points arranged as rows in a data matrix \(X\) (\(m \times n\)):

$$\hat{X} = X P$$
  • \(X\) (\(m \times n\)): The original data matrix where row \(i\) is \(\mathbf{x}_i^T\).
  • \(P\) (\(n \times n\)): The transformation matrix whose columns are the orthonormal basis vectors (e.g., eigenvectors in PCA).
  • \(\hat{X}\) (\(m \times n\)): The coordinates of all points in the new basis (often called scores or projected data).

Every row at once

Row \(i\) of \(X\) times \(P\) is row \(i\) of \(\hat X\).

Act 2 / Coordinates

Mean-Centering Property

“Each new column is a mix of centred columns, so \(\hat{X}\) is centred too.”

If the original data \(X\) has already been mean-centered (mean of each column is 0):

  • Each column of \(\hat{X}\) is formed by a linear combination of the columns of \(X\): $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{*k} = X \mathbf{p}_k$$
  • Because the expectation/mean operator is linear: $$\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{*k}] = \mathbb{E}[X] \mathbf{p}_k = \mathbf{0}^T \mathbf{p}_k = 0$$
  • Therefore, rotating or projecting already-centered data preserves zero mean in the new coordinate system without requiring re-centering.

Do two things move together?

  • More studying, less sleep: one rises as the other falls. Negative covariance.
  • No pattern: covariance near 0. In real data with millions of rows the pattern is not visible by eye, so we need a number.

Variance: spread around the mean

Each distance from the mean, squared, is a square. Variance is the average square: \(\mathrm{Var}(x) = \tfrac1m\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2\).

Two variances miss the trend

Mirror the points: \(\mathrm{Var}(x)\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(y)\) stay the same, but the trend reverses. We need a number that sees how \(x\) and \(y\) move together.

Covariance: one rectangle per point

\((x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)\) is the signed area of the rectangle from the mean to the point. Positive above-right and below-left, negative in the other two corners.

Act 2 / By hand

A covariance by hand

Data: \(a = 2, 4, 6, 8, 10\) and \(b = 1, 3, 4, 6, 8\). Means \(\bar a = 6\), \(\bar b = 4.4\).

$$\mathrm{Cov}(a, b) = \frac1m\sum_i (a_i - \bar a)(b_i - \bar b)$$

First point: \((2 - 6)(1 - 4.4) = (-4)(-3.4) = 13.6\). The other four products are \(2.8,\ 0,\ 3.2,\ 14.4\).

No product is negative: \(a\) and \(b\) are always on the same side of their means.

The five products sum to 34, so \(\mathrm{Cov}(a, b) = 34/5 = 6.8\). Positive: they rise and fall together.

Act 2 / Reading a covariance

Is a covariance of 6.8 big?

Hard to say. Its size depends on the units and on how spread out each variable is. Multiply every \(a\) by 5 and every \(b\) by 7: the covariance becomes \(35 \times 6.8 = 238\), for exactly the same relationship.

Act 2 / Correlation

Correlation: the average product of z-scores

  1. Subtract the mean: removes each variable's baseline.
  2. Divide by the standard deviation: removes its units and its spread. The results are z-scores, distances from the mean in standard units.
  3. Multiply the paired z-scores and average them.
$$\rho_{ab} = \frac1m\sum_i \frac{a_i - \bar a}{\sigma_a}\cdot\frac{b_i - \bar b}{\sigma_b} = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(a, b)}{\sigma_a\,\sigma_b}$$

Our example: \(\sigma_a = 2.83\), \(\sigma_b = 2.42\), so \(\rho = 6.8 / (2.83 \times 2.42) = 0.99\). Scaling \(a\) by 5 and \(b\) by 7 leaves \(\rho\) at 0.99.

Act 2 / Covariance matrix

All the variances and covariances in one matrix

$$C = \begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{Cov}(x,x) & \mathrm{Cov}(x,y)\\ \mathrm{Cov}(y,x) & \mathrm{Cov}(y,y)\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{Var}(x) & \mathrm{Cov}(x,y)\\ \mathrm{Cov}(x,y) & \mathrm{Var}(y)\end{bmatrix}$$
  • \(\mathrm{Cov}(x,x) = \mathrm{Var}(x)\), so the diagonal holds the variances. \(\mathrm{Cov}(x,y) = \mathrm{Cov}(y,x)\), so \(C\) is symmetric.
  • With \(n\) features, \(C\) is \(n \times n\). Entry \(jk\) pairs feature columns \(j\) and \(k\) of the centred \(X\) (means are 0):
$$C_{jk} = \frac1m\sum_i X_{ij}X_{ik} = \frac1m (X^TX)_{jk} \ \Longrightarrow\ C = \frac1m X^TX$$
  • np.cov divides by \(m - 1\). Same eigenvectors, eigenvalues scaled by \(m/(m-1)\).
Act 2 / Uses

Where covariance is used

Finance

Daily returns of Google and Meta rise together; the S&P 500 and a volatility index move opposite. Mixing assets with low covariance spreads risk.

Engineering

Quantities that interact, such as temperature and pressure in a gas, vary together. Their covariance goes into the model.

Machine learning

Which features move together, and which are redundant. PCA is built entirely on this.

Standardised data

Turn every column into z-scores and the covariance matrix becomes the correlation matrix. PCA on it treats all units alike.

Act 2 / Example

Four points, by hand

$$X = \begin{bmatrix}2&2\\-2&-2\\1&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}$$

Column sums are 0: already centred. \(m = 4\).

$$X^TX = \begin{bmatrix}4+4+1+1 & 4+4-1-1\\ \cdot & 4+4+1+1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}10&6\\6&10\end{bmatrix}$$

$$C = \tfrac14 X^TX = \begin{bmatrix}2.5&1.5\\1.5&2.5\end{bmatrix}$$

Both features have variance 2.5, and they move together (covariance 1.5). This example carries through the rest of the block.
