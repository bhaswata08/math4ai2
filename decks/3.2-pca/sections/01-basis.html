  • In \(x, y\) neither column is small, so neither can go.
  • After a change of basis one direction holds 94% of the variance. The other is close to noise.
  • A rotation keeps the total. It only moves variance from one direction to another.

PCA first transforms the data to a new basis, then drops the new directions with little variance.

The correlation between \(y\) and \(z\) is \(0.997\): when one goes up, so does the other, almost exactly.

  • \(z\) is almost a multiple of \(y\): the two columns are close to linearly dependent.
  • In practice: height in cm and in inches, salary and income tax.
  • Keeping both wastes a column. One new direction, roughly \(y + z\), carries the same information.
Act 1 / Goals

What we want from the new basis

Find orthonormal \(\mathbf p_1, \dots, \mathbf p_n\) such that, in the new coordinates:

No redundancy

The new coordinates are uncorrelated: covariance 0 for every pair.

No noise

Variance piles up in the first few directions. The rest carry almost none.

  • Orthonormal is the most convenient basis: coordinates are dot products (Block 1.2), and independence comes for free.
  • Then keep the first \(k \ll n\) coordinates and drop the others.
