Rotate, stretch, rotate

Read \(U\Sigma V^T\) from right to left and watch each factor move space.

The unit circle becomes an ellipse

\(A = \begin{bmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{bmatrix}\): \(V^T\) turns \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\) onto the axes, \(\Sigma\) stretches by \(6.71\) and \(2.24\), \(U\) turns the axes onto \(\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2\).

Perpendicular in, perpendicular out

\(A\mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i\). The right singular vectors are the one perpendicular pair that \(A\) keeps perpendicular.

Act 4 / Reading the picture

The ellipse holds the whole SVD

  • Its axes point along \(\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2\).
  • Its semi-axis lengths are \(\sigma_1, \sigma_2\).
  • The points of the circle that land on the axes are \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\).

\(\sigma_1\) is the most \(A\) can stretch a unit vector, \(\sigma_2\) the least.

Act 4 / 3D to 2D

Example: a \(2 \times 3\) matrix

\(A = \begin{bmatrix}3&2&2\\2&3&-2\end{bmatrix} \qquad AA^T = \begin{bmatrix}17&8\\8&17\end{bmatrix}\)

The characteristic equation is \((17-\lambda)^2 - 64 = 0\), so \(\lambda = 25,\ 9\).

\((AA^T - \lambda I)\mathbf{u} = 0\) gives \(\mathbf{u}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)\) for 25 and \(\mathbf{u}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)\) for 9. \(U\) rotates by 45 degrees.

\(\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i} = 5,\ 3\), so \(\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix}5&0&0\\0&3&0\end{bmatrix}\). The \(\mathbf{v}_i = A^T\mathbf{u}_i/\sigma_i\) are the first two rows of the \(3 \times 3\) rotation \(V^T\).

What \(A\) does in one go

The unit cube lands in the plane as a slanted hexagon. Hard to read in one step.

Step 1: rotate with \(V^T\)

The right singular vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3\) turn onto the \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) axes.

Step 2: erase \(z\), stretch with \(\Sigma\)

\(\Sigma\) drops the third axis, then stretches \(x\) by 5 and \(y\) by 3.

Step 3: rotate with \(U\)

The axes turn onto the left singular vectors \(\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2\). The result is exactly what \(A\) did in one go.

The same steps on a sphere

Step 1 again: \(V^T\) turns the sphere so the right singular vectors lie on the axes.

The sphere becomes an ellipse

Seen from above, the flattened sphere is a disc. \(\Sigma\) stretches it to semi-axes 5 and 3.

Rotate the ellipse into place

\(U\) turns the ellipse axes onto \(\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2\). A unit sphere always lands on an ellipse with semi-axes \(\sigma_i\).

Act 4 / 2D to 3D

Example: a \(3 \times 2\) matrix

\(A = \begin{bmatrix}2&1\\3&1\\0&1.8\end{bmatrix}\)

\(A^TA = \begin{bmatrix}13&5\\5&5.24\end{bmatrix}\): trace 18.24, determinant 43.12, so \(\lambda^2 - 18.24\lambda + 43.12 = 0\) and \(\lambda \approx 15.45,\ 2.79\).

\(\sigma_1 = 3.93\), \(\sigma_2 = 1.67\). \(\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix}3.93&0\\0&1.67\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\) is a dimension adder with stretches.

What \(A\) does in one go

Flat discs in the plane land on a tilted plane in 3D.

Step 1: rotate with \(V^T\)

A small 2D rotation, about 26 degrees, puts \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\) on the axes.

Step 2: stretch, then add a dimension

Stretch \(x\) by 3.93 and \(y\) by 1.67, then lay the result flat in 3D.

Step 3: rotate with \(U\) in 3D

A 3D rotation tilts the flat shape onto the plane spanned by \(\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2\).

Four steps, any matrix

Rotate by \(V^T\), stretch by the \(\sigma_i\), erase or add dimensions, rotate by \(U\).
