Act 3 of 6

Where \(U\), \(\Sigma\) and \(V\) come from

Turn any matrix into two symmetric ones, then use the spectral theorem.

\(AA^T\): a \(2 \times 2\) symmetric matrix

  • Take any \(2\times3\) matrix \(A\).
  • \(AA^T\) is \(2\times2\) and symmetric.
  • Symmetric means the spectral theorem applies: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors.

\(A^TA\): a \(3 \times 3\) symmetric matrix

  • The other order, \(A^TA\), is \(3\times3\).
  • Also symmetric, whatever \(A\) is.
  • Same trick, different size.

One matrix, two symmetric matrices

  • One rectangular matrix gives two square symmetric ones.
  • \(AA^T\) is \(m\times m\); \(A^TA\) is \(n\times n\).
  • We can diagonalize both. That is the plan.
Act 3 / Why symmetric

Two facts, one line each

  • Symmetric: \((A^TA)^T = A^T (A^T)^T = A^TA\).
  • Positive semi-definite: \(\mathbf{x}^TA^TA\,\mathbf{x} = \|A\mathbf{x}\|^2 \ge 0\).

So the spectral theorem applies: real eigenvalues \(\lambda_i \ge 0\) and perpendicular eigenvectors.

\(A^TA\) and \(AA^T\) share their nonzero eigenvalues.

Why: \(A^TA\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}\) gives \(AA^T(A\mathbf{v}) = A(\lambda\mathbf{v}) = \lambda(A\mathbf{v})\), and \(A\mathbf{v} \ne 0\) when \(\lambda \ne 0\).

Their eigenvectors

  • Name them \(S_L=AA^T\) and \(S_R=A^TA\).
  • \(\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2\) are eigenvectors of \(S_L\).
  • \(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\mathbf{v}_3\) are eigenvectors of \(S_R\).

Left and right singular vectors

  • \(\mathbf{u}_i\): left singular vectors of \(A\).
  • \(\mathbf{v}_i\): right singular vectors of \(A\).
  • Left and right refer to which side of \(\Sigma\) they sit on in \(U\Sigma V^T\).

Order the eigenvalues

  • Sort the eigenvalues from largest to smallest.
  • For our \(A = \begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\4&5&6\end{bmatrix}\): \(90.40\) and \(0.60\), shared by \(S_L\) and \(S_R\).
  • \(S_R\) has a third eigenvalue, 0, with no partner in \(S_L\).

Singular values

  • Singular value: \(\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}\).
  • For our \(A\): \(\sigma_1=9.51\), \(\sigma_2=0.77\).
  • They are never negative, and they are the stretch factors in \(\Sigma\).

The three factors

  • \(U\): orthogonal, columns \(\mathbf{u}_i\).
  • \(\Sigma\): rectangular diagonal, entries \(\sigma_i\).
  • \(V^T\): orthogonal, rows \(\mathbf{v}_i^T\).

The shapes of \(U\), \(\Sigma\), \(V^T\)

  • \(A\) is \(2\times3\).
  • \(U\) is \(2\times2\): eigenvectors of \(AA^T\).
  • \(\Sigma\) is \(2\times3\): eraser plus stretches.
  • \(V^T\) is \(3\times3\): eigenvectors of \(A^TA\).

Any shape works the same way

  • \(A\) is \(4\times2\): \(U\) is \(4\times4\), \(\Sigma\) is \(4\times2\), \(V^T\) is \(2\times2\).
  • \(\Sigma\) always has the shape of \(A\).
  • Its zero rows add dimensions.
Act 3 / Recipe

Computing an SVD by hand

  1. Form \(A^TA\). Find its eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge 0\).
  2. \(\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}\). The unit eigenvectors are the \(\mathbf{v}_i\).
  3. \(\mathbf{u}_i = A\mathbf{v}_i / \sigma_i\) for every \(\sigma_i > 0\).

Step 3 saves a second eigenproblem, and it fixes the signs of the \(\mathbf{u}_i\) to match the \(\mathbf{v}_i\).

Act 3 / Worked example

\(A = \begin{bmatrix}3&2\\2&3\\2&-2\end{bmatrix}\), a \(3\times2\) matrix

  • It takes 2 numbers in and gives 3 out, so there is no \(S = Q\Lambda Q^T\) for it.
  • \(A^TA\) is \(2\times2\) whatever the shape of \(A\). Its size is the number of columns.
  • So we follow the recipe: \(A^TA\), then \(\sigma_i\) and \(\mathbf{v}_i\), then \(\mathbf{u}_i\).
Act 3 / Worked example, step 1

Form \(A^TA\)

Entry \((i,j)\) of \(A^TA\) is column \(i\) of \(A\) dotted with column \(j\).

The columns are \(\mathbf{c}_1 = (3,2,2)\) and \(\mathbf{c}_2 = (2,3,-2)\).

  • \(\mathbf{c}_1\!\cdot\!\mathbf{c}_1 = 9 + 4 + 4 = 17\)
  • \(\mathbf{c}_1\!\cdot\!\mathbf{c}_2 = 6 + 6 - 4 = 8\)
  • \(\mathbf{c}_2\!\cdot\!\mathbf{c}_2 = 4 + 9 + 4 = 17\)
\(A^TA = \begin{bmatrix}17&8\\8&17\end{bmatrix}\)
Act 3 / Worked example, step 2

Eigenvalues of \(A^TA\)

Solve \(\det(A^TA - \lambda I) = 0\).

\(\det\begin{bmatrix}17-\lambda&8\\8&17-\lambda\end{bmatrix} = (17-\lambda)^2 - 64 = 0\)
  • \(17 - \lambda = \pm 8\), so \(\lambda_1 = 25\) and \(\lambda_2 = 9\).
  • Check: trace \(= 17 + 17 = 34 = 25 + 9\), determinant \(= 289 - 64 = 225 = 25\cdot 9\).

\(\sigma_1 = \sqrt{25} = 5\), \(\sigma_2 = \sqrt{9} = 3\).

Act 3 / Worked example, step 3

Eigenvectors of \(A^TA\)

  • \(\lambda = 25\): \(\begin{bmatrix}-8&8\\8&-8\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{v} = 0\). Both rows say \(-x + y = 0\), so \(y = x\).
  • \(\lambda = 9\): \(\begin{bmatrix}8&8\\8&8\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{v} = 0\). Both rows say \(x + y = 0\), so \(y = -x\).
  • Divide each by its length \(\sqrt2\) to get unit vectors:
\(\mathbf{v}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)\), \(\mathbf{v}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)\)

\(\mathbf{v}_1\cdot\mathbf{v}_2 = 0\), as it must be: \(A^TA\) is symmetric.

Act 3 / Worked example, step 4

The \(\mathbf{u}_i\): send \(\mathbf{v}_i\) through \(A\)

\(A\mathbf{v}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}3+2\\2+3\\2-2\end{bmatrix} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(5,5,0)\). Its length is \(\sqrt{25} = 5 = \sigma_1\).

\(\mathbf{u}_1 = \dfrac{A\mathbf{v}_1}{5} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1,0)\).

\(A\mathbf{v}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}3-2\\2-3\\2+2\end{bmatrix} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1,4)\). Its length is \(\sqrt{9} = 3 = \sigma_2\).

\(\mathbf{u}_2 = \dfrac{A\mathbf{v}_2}{3} = \tfrac{1}{3\sqrt2}(1,-1,4)\).

Act 3 / Worked example, step 4

Why \(\|A\mathbf{v}_i\| = \sigma_i\)

\(\|A\mathbf{v}_i\|^2 = \mathbf{v}_i^TA^TA\,\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{v}_i^T(\lambda_i\mathbf{v}_i) = \lambda_i\)

So \(\|A\mathbf{v}_i\| = \sqrt{\lambda_i} = \sigma_i\), and dividing by \(\sigma_i\) always leaves a unit vector.

\(\mathbf{u}_1\cdot\mathbf{u}_2 = \tfrac{1}{6}(1 - 1 + 0) = 0\). The \(\mathbf{u}_i\) come out perpendicular without being asked.

Act 3 / Worked example, step 5

The missing third direction

  • \(U\) is \(3\times3\) but we have only \(\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2\). Both live in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).
  • \(\mathbf{u}_3\) must be perpendicular to both: \(x + y = 0\) and \(x - y + 4z = 0\).
  • That gives \(y = -x\), then \(2x + 4z = 0\), so \(z = -x/2\). Scale to unit length:
\(\mathbf{u}_3 = \tfrac13(2,-2,-1)\)

No \(\sigma_3\) goes with it. It is the direction in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) that \(A\) never reaches.

Act 3 / Worked example

Put it together

\(\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\2&3\\2&-2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}&\tfrac{1}{3\sqrt2}&\tfrac23\\[2pt]\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}&-\tfrac{1}{3\sqrt2}&-\tfrac23\\[2pt]0&\tfrac{4}{3\sqrt2}&-\tfrac13\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\0&3\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\)

Check the top-left entry: \(5\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} + 3\cdot\tfrac{1}{3\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} = \tfrac52 + \tfrac12 = 3\).

Check the bottom-right entry: \(3\cdot\tfrac{4}{3\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac{-1}{\sqrt2} = -2\).

\(\Sigma\) is \(3\times2\), with a zero row under the \(\sigma_i\). Shapes: \(3\times3\) times \(3\times2\) times \(2\times2\).

Act 3 / Properties

What the SVD tells you

  • Rank = the number of nonzero \(\sigma_i\).
  • Largest stretch: \(\max_{\|\mathbf{x}\|=1}\|A\mathbf{x}\| = \sigma_1\), reached at \(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{v}_1\).
  • Inverse (square, all \(\sigma_i > 0\)): \(A^{-1} = V\Sigma^{-1}U^T\).
  • Symmetric PSD \(S\): the SVD is the spectral decomposition, \(U = V = Q\) and \(\Sigma = \Lambda\).
  • Symmetric with a negative \(\lambda\): \(\sigma = |\lambda|\), and the sign moves into \(U\).
