Where \(U\), \(\Sigma\) and \(V\) come from

Turn any matrix into two symmetric ones, then use the spectral theorem.

\(AA^T\): a \(2 \times 2\) symmetric matrix

  • Take any \(2\times3\) matrix \(A\).
  • \(AA^T\) is \(2\times2\) and symmetric.
  • Symmetric means the spectral theorem applies: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors.

\(A^TA\): a \(3 \times 3\) symmetric matrix

  • The other order, \(A^TA\), is \(3\times3\).
  • Also symmetric, whatever \(A\) is.
  • Same trick, different size.

One matrix, two symmetric matrices

  • One rectangular matrix gives two square symmetric ones.
  • \(AA^T\) is \(m\times m\); \(A^TA\) is \(n\times n\).
  • We can diagonalize both. That is the plan.
Act 3 / Why symmetric

Two facts, one line each

  • Symmetric: \((A^TA)^T = A^T (A^T)^T = A^TA\).
  • Positive semi-definite: \(\mathbf{x}^TA^TA\,\mathbf{x} = \|A\mathbf{x}\|^2 \ge 0\).

So the spectral theorem applies: real eigenvalues \(\lambda_i \ge 0\) and perpendicular eigenvectors.

\(A^TA\) and \(AA^T\) share their nonzero eigenvalues.

Why: \(A^TA\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}\) gives \(AA^T(A\mathbf{v}) = A(\lambda\mathbf{v}) = \lambda(A\mathbf{v})\), and \(A\mathbf{v} \ne 0\) when \(\lambda \ne 0\).

Their eigenvectors

  • Name them \(S_L=AA^T\) and \(S_R=A^TA\).
  • \(\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2\) are eigenvectors of \(S_L\).
  • \(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\mathbf{v}_3\) are eigenvectors of \(S_R\).

Left and right singular vectors

  • \(\mathbf{u}_i\): left singular vectors of \(A\).
  • \(\mathbf{v}_i\): right singular vectors of \(A\).
  • Left and right refer to which side of \(\Sigma\) they sit on in \(U\Sigma V^T\).

Order the eigenvalues

  • Sort the eigenvalues from largest to smallest.
  • For our \(A\): \(90.40\) and \(0.60\), shared by \(S_L\) and \(S_R\).
  • \(S_R\) has a third eigenvalue, 0, with no partner in \(S_L\).

Singular values

  • Singular value: \(\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}\).
  • For our \(A\): \(\sigma_1=9.51\), \(\sigma_2=0.77\).
  • They are never negative, and they are the stretch factors in \(\Sigma\).

The three factors

  • \(U\): orthogonal, columns \(\mathbf{u}_i\).
  • \(\Sigma\): rectangular diagonal, entries \(\sigma_i\).
  • \(V^T\): orthogonal, rows \(\mathbf{v}_i^T\).

The shapes of \(U\), \(\Sigma\), \(V^T\)

  • \(A\) is \(2\times3\).
  • \(U\) is \(2\times2\): eigenvectors of \(AA^T\).
  • \(\Sigma\) is \(2\times3\): eraser plus stretches.
  • \(V^T\) is \(3\times3\): eigenvectors of \(A^TA\).

Any shape works the same way

  • \(A\) is \(4\times2\): \(U\) is \(4\times4\), \(\Sigma\) is \(4\times2\), \(V^T\) is \(2\times2\).
  • \(\Sigma\) always has the shape of \(A\).
  • Its zero rows add dimensions.
Act 3 / Recipe

Computing an SVD by hand

  1. Form \(A^TA\). Find its eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge 0\).
  2. \(\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}\). The unit eigenvectors are the \(\mathbf{v}_i\).
  3. \(\mathbf{u}_i = A\mathbf{v}_i / \sigma_i\) for every \(\sigma_i > 0\).

Step 3 saves a second eigenproblem, and it fixes the signs of the \(\mathbf{u}_i\) to match the \(\mathbf{v}_i\).

Act 3 / Worked example

\(A = \begin{bmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{bmatrix}\)

  1. \(A^TA = \begin{bmatrix}25&20\\20&25\end{bmatrix}\). Trace 50, determinant 225, so \(\lambda^2 - 50\lambda + 225 = (\lambda-45)(\lambda-5) = 0\).
  2. \(\lambda = 45\): \(\begin{bmatrix}-20&20\\20&-20\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{v} = 0 \Rightarrow y = x\). \(\lambda = 5\): \(\begin{bmatrix}20&20\\20&20\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{v} = 0 \Rightarrow y = -x\).
  3. \(\sigma_1 = \sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt5\), \(\sigma_2 = \sqrt5\). Unit eigenvectors \(\mathbf{v}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)\), \(\mathbf{v}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(-1, 1)\).
  4. \(\mathbf{u}_1 = \dfrac{A\mathbf{v}_1}{3\sqrt5} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(1, 3)\), \(\mathbf{u}_2 = \dfrac{A\mathbf{v}_2}{\sqrt5} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(-3, 1)\).
Act 3 / Worked example

Put it together

\(\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{bmatrix} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-3\\3&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3\sqrt5&0\\0&\sqrt5\end{bmatrix}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}\)

Check one entry: row 1 of \(U\Sigma\) is \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(3\sqrt5,\ -3\sqrt5)\). Times column 1 of \(V^T\), \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1)\), gives \(\tfrac{6\sqrt5}{\sqrt{20}} = 3\).

\(A\) is not symmetric, yet both \(U\) and \(V\) are rotations.

Act 3 / Properties

What the SVD tells you

  • Rank = the number of nonzero \(\sigma_i\).
  • Largest stretch: \(\max_{\|\mathbf{x}\|=1}\|A\mathbf{x}\| = \sigma_1\), reached at \(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{v}_1\).
  • Inverse (square, all \(\sigma_i > 0\)): \(A^{-1} = V\Sigma^{-1}U^T\).
  • Symmetric PSD \(S\): the SVD is the spectral decomposition, \(U = V = Q\) and \(\Sigma = \Lambda\).
  • Symmetric with a negative \(\lambda\): \(\sigma = |\lambda|\), and the sign moves into \(U\).
