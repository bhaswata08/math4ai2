Matrices that change dimension

A rectangular matrix takes vectors from one space to a space of a different size.

From \(\mathbb{R}^3\) to \(\mathbb{R}^2\)

  • Input has 3 entries, output has 2.
  • Columns count the inputs, rows count the outputs.
  • So the matrix has 2 rows and 3 columns.

Three numbers in, two numbers out

  • Each output entry is one row dotted with the input.
  • Two rows give two numbers, so \(\mathbb{R}^3\) lands in \(\mathbb{R}^2\).
  • Check the first entry: \(3 - 4 + 3 = 2\).
Act 2 / Shapes

An \(m \times n\) matrix maps \(\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m\)

  • Columns count the inputs: \(n\).
  • Rows count the outputs: \(m\).
  • A linear layer \(\mathbf{y} = W\mathbf{x}\) with \(W\) of shape \(256 \times 784\) maps a 784-pixel image to 256 features.

Wide matrices lose a dimension. Tall matrices add one.

A cube in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

  • A cube is a stack of unit blocks, one vector per corner.
  • A \(3\times3\) matrix such as \(2I\) keeps the cube in 3D.
  • It can grow, rotate or shear the cube but not flatten it, unless it is singular.

Flatten a cube

  • A \(2\times3\) matrix has only two output coordinates.
  • The whole cube is pressed onto a plane.
  • Many 3D points share one image: information is lost.

The dimension eraser

  • The rows pick out \(x\) and \(y\).
  • The third column is zero, so \(z\) never reaches the output.
  • This is the dimension eraser.

Every vector drops onto the plane

  • \((x,y,z)\mapsto(x,y)\): every vector drops straight down.
  • Points at different heights land on the same spot.
  • The height is gone and cannot be recovered.

A torus, a cube, a cylinder

  • Same eraser, three solids.
  • Torus becomes a ring, cube a square, cylinder a disc.
  • Shadow on the floor: the eraser is a projection.

The dimension adder

  • A \(3\times2\) matrix takes 2 numbers in and gives 3 out.
  • The zero row sets \(z=0\).
  • This is the dimension adder, the transpose of the eraser.

Flat shapes lifted into 3D

  • The adder changes no shape, it only moves it into 3D.
  • Disc, square and hexagon now lie in the plane \(z=0\).
  • The output is still only a 2D plane inside \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

Erase, then stretch

  • Erase first, then stretch: multiply the two matrices.
  • The product is rectangular and diagonal.
  • This shape is exactly what \(\Sigma\) will have.

A cylinder, step by step

  • Erase the height: the cylinder becomes a disc.
  • Stretch \(x\) by 4 and \(y\) by 2: the disc becomes an ellipse.
  • The single product matrix does both at once.

Rectangular diagonal matrices

  1. What does \(\begin{bmatrix}3&0&0\\0&0.5&0\end{bmatrix}\) do to \((x, y, z)\)? \((3x,\ 0.5y)\): drop \(z\), stretch \(x\) by 3, shrink \(y\) by half.
  2. Write the \(3 \times 2\) matrix that stretches \(x\) by 2, \(y\) by 5 and places the result in 3D.

This is the shape \(\Sigma\) will have: a stretch on the diagonal, padded with zeros.
