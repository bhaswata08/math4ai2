Act 2 of 5

The Hessian

Fit a parabola to the loss, jump to its lowest point, repeat. That is Newton-Raphson, and the matrix it needs is the Hessian.

Act 2 / Newton-Raphson in one variable

Write the whole series, then cut it

We are at \(\omega_0\) and want a step \(\delta\) that lowers the loss \(L\). Act 1 gives the whole Taylor series of \(L\) around \(\omega_0\), written in \(\delta\):

$$L(\omega_0 + \delta) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{L^{(k)}(\omega_0)}{k!}\,\delta^k = L(\omega_0) + L'(\omega_0)\,\delta + \tfrac12\,L''(\omega_0)\,\delta^2 + \tfrac16\,L'''(\omega_0)\,\delta^3 + \cdots$$

The step is small, so \(\delta^3\) and everything after it is smaller still. We cut the series off after the quadratic term and keep:

$$L(\omega_0 + \delta) \approx L(\omega_0) + L'(\omega_0)\,\delta + \tfrac12\,L''(\omega_0)\,\delta^2$$

Three numbers we can compute at \(\omega_0\), and a parabola in \(\delta\). We know where a parabola's bottom is.

Act 2 / Newton-Raphson in one variable

Ask the parabola where its bottom is

$$L(\omega_0 + \delta) \approx L(\omega_0) + L'(\omega_0)\,\delta + \tfrac12\,L''(\omega_0)\,\delta^2$$

Differentiate the right-hand side with respect to \(\delta\), set that to zero, and solve. Only the last two terms survive, since \(L(\omega_0)\) is a constant:

$$L'(\omega_0) + L''(\omega_0)\,\delta = 0$$
$$\delta^{*} = -\,\frac{L'(\omega_0)}{L''(\omega_0)}$$
$$\omega_{1} = \omega_0 - \frac{L'(\omega_0)}{L''(\omega_0)}$$

No learning rate appears. The second derivative sets the step length for us.

Act 2 / Reading the step

Curvature decides how far to move

Steep bowl

\(L''\) large: dividing by it gives a short, careful step. The minimum is close.

Flat ground

\(L''\) small: the step is long. The slope will not change much on the way.

Curving down

\(L'' < 0\): the parabola has no bottom, and the step walks to a maximum instead.

On \(L(\omega) = \omega^2\) the step is \(-2\omega_0/2 = -\omega_0\): one jump to the minimum, exactly, because the quadratic model is the function. On anything else the model is only local, so you take the step, refit, and repeat.

The sign of the curvature decides whether the Newton step is even pointing downhill. Act 3 turns that into a test.

Act 2 / One variable to many

The same step with \(p\) parameters

Now \(\boldsymbol\omega \in \mathbb R^p\) and the step \(\boldsymbol\delta\) is a vector too. The slope becomes the gradient \(\nabla L(\boldsymbol\omega)\), a vector of \(p\) first derivatives, and the curvature becomes the Hessian \(H(\boldsymbol\omega)\), the \(p \times p\) matrix of second derivatives \(H_{ij} = \partial^2 L / \partial \omega_i \partial \omega_j\). Every term keeps its shape:

$$L(\boldsymbol\omega + \boldsymbol\delta) \approx L(\boldsymbol\omega) + \nabla L(\boldsymbol\omega)^{\top}\boldsymbol\delta + \tfrac12\,\boldsymbol\delta^{\top} H(\boldsymbol\omega)\,\boldsymbol\delta$$

Minimise the right-hand side over \(\boldsymbol\delta\) and the division by \(L''\) becomes a solve against \(H\):

$$\boldsymbol\omega_{1} = \boldsymbol\omega_0 - H(\boldsymbol\omega_0)^{-1}\,\nabla L(\boldsymbol\omega_0)$$

For smooth \(L\) the mixed partials agree, so \(H\) is symmetric: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors. With \(p\) in the millions nobody forms \(H\), let alone inverts it, which is why Block 4.3 goes back to a learning rate.

Act 2 / Where you cut the series

Cut after the linear term, cut after the quadratic

Both optimisers in this course are the same Taylor series, truncated in different places. For a scalar loss the Jacobian of \(L\) is the \(1 \times p\) row of first derivatives, and \(\nabla L\) is its transpose.

First order: gradient descent

$$L(\boldsymbol\omega_0 + \boldsymbol\delta) \approx L(\boldsymbol\omega_0) + \nabla L(\boldsymbol\omega_0)^{\top}\boldsymbol\delta$$

A plane has no bottom: pushing \(\boldsymbol\delta\) along \(-\nabla L\) lowers it forever. The model gives a direction and nothing else, so you pick the step length yourself.

$$\boldsymbol\omega_1 = \boldsymbol\omega_0 - \eta\,\nabla L(\boldsymbol\omega_0)$$

Second order: Newton

$$L(\boldsymbol\omega_0 + \boldsymbol\delta) \approx L(\boldsymbol\omega_0) + \nabla L(\boldsymbol\omega_0)^{\top}\boldsymbol\delta + \tfrac12\,\boldsymbol\delta^{\top} H \boldsymbol\delta$$

A bowl has a bottom. Setting the derivative in \(\boldsymbol\delta\) to zero solves for it, and the curvature fixes the step length.

$$\boldsymbol\omega_1 = \boldsymbol\omega_0 - H(\boldsymbol\omega_0)^{-1}\nabla L(\boldsymbol\omega_0)$$

\(\eta\) is the price of throwing the curvature away. Block 4.3 spends an act on choosing it.

From slices to a quadratic

Take \(f(x, y) = x^3 + 2xy - y^2\) at \(\boldsymbol\omega = (1,1)\): slope 5 along \(x\), none along \(y\). The gradient gives the tangent plane, the Hessian bends it.

Act 2 / Worked example

\(f(x, y) = x^3 + 2xy - y^2\) at \(\boldsymbol\omega = (1,1)\)

$$\nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} 3x^2 + 2y \\ 2x - 2y \end{bmatrix}$$
$$H = \begin{bmatrix} 6x & 2 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$$
$$\nabla f(1,1) = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
$$H(1,1) = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$$

\(f(\boldsymbol\omega) = 2\), so \(f(\boldsymbol\omega + \boldsymbol\delta) \approx 2 + 5\delta_1 + 3\delta_1^2 + 2\delta_1\delta_2 - \delta_2^2\). The two off-diagonal entries of \(H\) are equal, as promised.
