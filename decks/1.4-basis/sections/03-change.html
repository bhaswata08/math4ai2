Changing basis

Jennifer and we describe the same arrows with different numbers.

Jennifer's grid

Different grid lines. Her first steps are \(\vec b_1 = [2, 1]\) and \(\vec b_2 = [-1, 1]\). The only point we agree on is the origin \([0, 0]\): we both get it by scaling our basis vectors by 0.

From her numbers to ours

Jennifer says \([-1, 2]\). Build \(-1\,\vec b_1 + 2\,\vec b_2\) on her grid, then read the tip on ours.

Act 3 / Her numbers to ours

Scale her basis vectors, then add

$$-1\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} + 2\textcolor{#c792ea}{\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 - 2 \\ -1 + 2 \end{bmatrix} = \textcolor{#f0a35e}{\begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}$$

Her \([-1, 2]\) is our \([-4, 1]\). Day 2 packs this recipe into the change-of-basis matrix.

From our numbers to hers

We say \([3, 2]\). Find \(a, b\) with \(a\,\vec b_1 + b\,\vec b_2 = [3, 2]\).

Act 3 / Our numbers to hers

Two equations, two unknowns

$$a\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} + b\textcolor{#c792ea}{\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \;\Longrightarrow\; \begin{aligned} 2a - b &= 3 \\ a + b &= 2 \end{aligned}$$
$$3a = 5 \;\Rightarrow\; \textcolor{#f0a35e}{a = \tfrac53},\quad \textcolor{#f0a35e}{b = 2 - \tfrac53 = \tfrac13}$$

Check: \(\tfrac53[2,1] + \tfrac13[-1,1] = [3, 2]\). Her coordinates are \((\tfrac53, \tfrac13)\).

Act 3 / We will come back to this

Both translations are a matrix

$$\begin{bmatrix} \textcolor{#6fd0c0}{2} & \textcolor{#c792ea}{-1} \\ \textcolor{#6fd0c0}{1} & \textcolor{#c792ea}{1} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \textcolor{#f0a35e}{\begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}$$

Put her basis vectors in as columns. This matrix turns her numbers into ours, and its inverse turns ours into hers.

We revisit this properly with matrices, as the change-of-basis matrix.

Act 3 / A better basis

Orthonormal: coordinates are dot products

Let \(\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1}, \textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2}\) be an orthonormal basis: unit length and at right angles,

$$\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1}\cdot\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1} = \textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2}\cdot\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2} = 1,\qquad \textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1}\cdot\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2} = 0$$

Being a basis, they can build any \(\vec x\):

$$\vec x = c_1\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1} + c_2\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2}$$

Dot both sides with \(\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1}\), then with \(\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2}\):

$$\begin{aligned} \vec x\cdot\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1} &= c_1\underbrace{\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1}\cdot\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1}}_{1} + c_2\underbrace{\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2}\cdot\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1}}_{0} = c_1 \\ \vec x\cdot\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2} &= c_1\underbrace{\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1}\cdot\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2}}_{0} + c_2\underbrace{\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2}\cdot\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2}}_{1} = c_2 \end{aligned}$$

Substituting back into \(\vec x = c_1\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1} + c_2\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2}\): \(\vec x = (\vec x\cdot\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1})\,\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1} + (\vec x\cdot\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2})\,\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2}\). No equations to solve.

Act 3 / A better basis

The same \(\vec x = [3, 2]\), two dot products

$$\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 \\ 0.8 \end{bmatrix}},\qquad \textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -0.8 \\ 0.6 \end{bmatrix}}$$
$$\begin{aligned} c_1 &= \vec x\cdot\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\vec u_1} = 3(0.6) + 2(0.8) = \textcolor{#f0a35e}{3.4} \\ c_2 &= \vec x\cdot\textcolor{#c792ea}{\vec u_2} = 3(-0.8) + 2(0.6) = \textcolor{#f0a35e}{-1.2} \end{aligned}$$
$$\text{Check: }\ \textcolor{#f0a35e}{3.4}\textcolor{#6fd0c0}{\begin{bmatrix} 0.6 \\ 0.8 \end{bmatrix}} \textcolor{#f0a35e}{-\,1.2}\textcolor{#c792ea}{\begin{bmatrix} -0.8 \\ 0.6 \end{bmatrix}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.04 + 0.96 \\ 2.72 - 0.72 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Jennifer's basis needed two equations solved together for this \(\vec x\). Here each coordinate is one dot product, worked out on its own.
