Act 3 / Spectral theorem

The spectral theorem

A real symmetric \(S\) has

  • real eigenvalues
  • orthonormal eigenvectors
$$S = Q\,\Lambda\,Q^{T}, \qquad Q^{-1} = Q^{T}$$

No inverse to compute: the inverse of \(Q\) is its transpose.

Orthogonal, diagonal, orthogonal

The spectral decomposition: a rotation, a stretch along the axes, and the rotation back.

Choose \(Q\) to be a rotation

\(Q\) turns the standard basis onto the eigenvectors; \(Q^{-1} = Q^T\) turns them back. If \(\det Q = -1\), flip the sign of one eigenvector, or \(Q\) also reflects.

Column \(i\) of \(Q\) is eigenvector \(i\)

The \(i\)-th standard basis vector lands on the \(i\)-th eigenvector. The clip writes \(Q^{-1}\); for an orthogonal \(Q\) that is \(Q^T\).

What \(S\) does in 2D

\(S = \begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}\) turns the rectangle into a parallelogram.

Rotate, scale, rotate back

\(Q^T\) aligns the eigenvectors with the axes, \(\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(6,2)\) scales them, \(Q\) rotates back.

Act 3 / Check

The same \(S\), numerically

\(\lambda_1 = 6\), \(\mathbf{q}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)\); \(\lambda_2 = 2\), \(\mathbf{q}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)\).

$$S = \sum_i \lambda_i\,\mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^{T} = 6\cdot\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix} + 2\cdot\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}$$

A symmetric matrix is a sum of rank-one pieces, one per eigenvector.

The same in 3D

A \(3\times3\) symmetric \(S\) deforms the cube. Now take it apart in three steps.

Step 1: rotate with \(Q^T\)

The three eigenvectors land on the \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) axes.

Step 2: scale with \(\Lambda\)

Each axis is scaled by its eigenvalue. A negative one also flips that axis.

Step 3: rotate back with \(Q\)

The result is exactly what \(S\) did to the cube.
