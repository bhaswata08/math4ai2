Diagonalization

Most matrices are not symmetric. If \(A\) still has \(n\) independent eigenvectors, it splits into three moves the same way. The eigenvectors are just no longer perpendicular.

Act 5 / Statement

\(A = PDP^{-1}\)

Let \(A\) be \(n\times n\) with independent eigenvectors \(\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n\) and eigenvalues \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n\).

  • \(P = [\,\mathbf{v}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{v}_n\,]\): eigenvectors as columns
  • \(D = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)\): eigenvalues in the same order

Compare with Act 4: \(Q\) becomes \(P\), \(\Lambda\) becomes \(D\), and \(Q^T\) becomes \(P^{-1}\), since \(P\) is not orthogonal.

This is the \(B^{-1}MB\) of Block 2.2 with the eigenvectors as the basis: \(P^{-1}AP = D\).

Invertible, diagonal, invertible

  • Built right to left: the order in which the moves act.
  • \(P^{-1}\): eigenvectors to axes.
  • \(D\): stretch the axes.
  • \(P\): axes back to eigenvectors.
  • Compare \(S = Q\Lambda Q^T\): rotation, stretch, rotation.

What goes into \(P\) and \(D\)

  • Columns of \(P\): eigenvectors of \(A\), any length.
  • Diagonal of \(D\): eigenvalues, in the same order.
  • Last factor: \(P^{-1}\), not \(P^T\).
  • \(3\times3\): three of each.
Act 5 / Why it holds

From \(A\mathbf{v}_i = \lambda_i\mathbf{v}_i\) to \(A = PDP^{-1}\)

Multiply \(A\) into \(P\) one column at a time:

$$AP = [\,A\mathbf{v}_1\ \ A\mathbf{v}_2\,] = [\,\lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1\ \ \lambda_2\mathbf{v}_2\,]$$

Scaling column \(i\) of \(P\) by \(\lambda_i\) is what multiplying by \(D\) on the right does:

$$[\,\lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1\ \ \lambda_2\mathbf{v}_2\,] = [\,\mathbf{v}_1\ \ \mathbf{v}_2\,]\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1&0\\0&\lambda_2\end{bmatrix} = PD$$

So \(AP = PD\). The columns of \(P\) are the eigenvectors \(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2\), which are independent, so \(P^{-1}\) exists. Multiply both sides on the right by \(P^{-1}\):

\(A = PDP^{-1}\)

\(P\mathbf{e}_1 = \mathbf{v}_1\), \(P\mathbf{e}_2 = \mathbf{v}_2\)

  • \(P\) times \((1,0)\) is its first column: \(\mathbf{v}_1\).
  • \(P\) times \((0,1)\) is \(\mathbf{v}_2\).
  • So \(P\) sends \(\mathbf{e}_i\) to \(\mathbf{v}_i\), as \(Q\) did.
  • \(P^{-1}\) sends \(\mathbf{v}_i\) back to \(\mathbf{e}_i\).

\(P\) is not a rotation

  • \(Q\) turned the grid rigidly.
  • \(P\) carries \(\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2\) to \(\mathbf{v}_1 = (1,1)\), \(\mathbf{v}_2 = (-1,2)\).
  • Lengths change and the right angle is lost.
  • So \(P\) also stretches and shears.

Our \(A\), factored

  • \(\mathbf{v}_1 = (1,1)\) with 5, \(\mathbf{v}_2 = (-1,2)\) with 2.
  • \(\mathbf{v}_2\) is \(-1\) times Act 3's \((1,-2)\): still an eigenvector.
  • This sign gives \(\det P = 3 > 0\): no flip, like choosing \(Q\) a rotation.

What \(A\) does in 2D

  • Apply \(A\) to the rectangle in one go.
  • It becomes a parallelogram.
  • Next: the same result in three moves.

Eigenvectors to axes, scale, back

  • \(P^{-1}\): eigenvectors onto the axes. It also shrinks, unlike \(Q^T\).
  • \(D\): scale \(x\) by 5, then \(y\) by 2.
  • \(P\): axes back onto the eigenvectors.
  • Gold: \(A\) in one go lands on the same shape.

Follow one vector

  • Follow \(\mathbf{x} = (3,0)\).
  • \(P^{-1}\mathbf{x} = (2,-1)\), so \(\mathbf{x} = 2\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2\).
  • \(D\): \((10,-2)\).
  • \(P\): \((12,6) = A\mathbf{x}\), the same point \(A\) gives directly.

The same in 3D

  • \(A = P\,\mathrm{diag}(3,-1,2)\,P^{-1}\)
  • Eigenvectors \((1,1,0)\), \((0,1,1)\), \((1,0,1)\): not perpendicular.
  • \(\det A = -6 < 0\): the cube turns inside out on the way.

Three moves in 3D

  • \(P^{-1}\): the three eigenvectors onto the axes.
  • \(D\): \(x\) by 3, \(y\) by \(-1\) (a flip), \(z\) by 2.
  • \(P\): back onto the eigenvectors.
  • Gold: \(A\) in one go, the same box.
Act 5 / Powers

\(A^k = PD^kP^{-1}\)

Write \(A^2\) out and cancel the middle pair:

$$A^2 = (PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1}) = PD(P^{-1}P)DP^{-1} = PD^2P^{-1}$$

Each new copy of \(A\) adds one more \(D\), so \(A^k = PD^kP^{-1}\) with \(D^k = \begin{bmatrix}5^k&0\\0&2^k\end{bmatrix}\).

Spot check, \(k = 3\): entry \((1,1)\) of \(P\,\mathrm{diag}(125,8)\,P^{-1}\) is \((125\cdot2 + (-8)(-1))/3 = 86\), the \((1,1)\) entry of \(A^3\).

Act 5 / Why it works

The biggest eigenvalue wins

Question: apply the same \(A\) again and again (a process repeated step after step). What does \(A^k\mathbf{x}\) look like for large \(k\)? This long-run behavior is what we read off from the eigenvalues, and it is how a dominant eigenvector is computed in practice.

Setup: \(A\) has eigenvectors \(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2\) with \(A\mathbf{v}_1=\lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1\), \(A\mathbf{v}_2=\lambda_2\mathbf{v}_2\). They form a basis, so any vector is \(\mathbf{x} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2\). Apply \(A\) once; matrices respect sums and scalings, so

$$A\mathbf{x} = c_1A\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2A\mathbf{v}_2 = c_1\lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\lambda_2\mathbf{v}_2$$

Each part is only scaled, by its own eigenvalue. Apply \(A\) to this result and each part is scaled again:

$$A^2\mathbf{x} = c_1\lambda_1^2\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\lambda_2^2\mathbf{v}_2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A^k\mathbf{x} = c_1\lambda_1^k\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\lambda_2^k\mathbf{v}_2$$

Act 5 / Why it works

Which part is left?

Compare the two parts of \(A^k\mathbf{x} = c_1\lambda_1^k\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\lambda_2^k\mathbf{v}_2\) with \(\lambda_1 = 5\), \(\lambda_2 = 2\). Divide both by \(5^k\), which changes the length but not the direction:

$$\frac{A^k\mathbf{x}}{5^k} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\left(\tfrac{2}{5}\right)^k\mathbf{v}_2$$

The \(\mathbf{v}_2\) coefficient is \(c_2\) times \(0.4,\ 0.16,\ 0.064,\ \dots\), which shrinks to \(0\). Only \(c_1\mathbf{v}_1\) is left.

In general, if \(|\lambda_1| > |\lambda_2|\) then \((\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^k \to 0\), so \(A^k\mathbf{x}\) points along \(\mathbf{v}_1\) (needs \(c_1 \neq 0\)).

Act 5 / Try it

Diagonalize and take powers

  1. For \(A=\begin{bmatrix}5&4\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\), write \(P\) and \(D\) and find \(A^{10}\)'s eigenvalues.
  2. Why can \(\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) not be diagonalized?
Act 5 / Solution 1

Step 1: eigenvalues

\(A=\begin{bmatrix}5&4\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\). Solve \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\):

$$\det\begin{bmatrix}5-\lambda&4\\1&2-\lambda\end{bmatrix} = (5-\lambda)(2-\lambda) - 4 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 6$$

Factor: \(\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 6 = (\lambda-6)(\lambda-1)\), so \(\lambda_1 = 6\) and \(\lambda_2 = 1\).

Check: the sum \(6+1 = 7\) is the trace, the product \(6\cdot 1 = 6\) is \(\det A\).

Act 5 / Solution 1

Step 2: eigenvectors

For \(\lambda_1 = 6\), solve \((A-6I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\):

$$\begin{bmatrix}-1&4\\1&-4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; x = 4y \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}4\\1\end{bmatrix}$$

For \(\lambda_2 = 1\), solve \((A-I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\):

$$\begin{bmatrix}4&4\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; x = -y \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}$$

Check: \(A\mathbf{v}_1 = (24, 6) = 6\mathbf{v}_1\) and \(A\mathbf{v}_2 = (-1, 1) = 1\cdot\mathbf{v}_2\).

Act 5 / Solution 1

Step 3: P, D, and the powers

Put the eigenvectors in the columns of \(P\) and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of \(D\), in the same order:

$$P = \begin{bmatrix}4&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad D = \begin{bmatrix}6&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad A = PDP^{-1}$$

Then \(A^{10} = PD^{10}P^{-1}\) with

$$D^{10} = \begin{bmatrix}6^{10}&0\\0&1^{10}\end{bmatrix}$$

\(A^{10}\) has the same eigenvectors as \(A\), and its eigenvalues are \(6^{10} = 60466176\) and \(1\).
