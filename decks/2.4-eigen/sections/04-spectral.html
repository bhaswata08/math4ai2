Spectral decomposition

When \(S = S^T\), the eigenvectors come out perpendicular, and \(S\) splits into a rotation, a stretch and a rotation back.

Usually not at a right angle

  • Take a matrix that is not symmetric.
  • Its two eigenvector lines meet at some angle.
  • In all four examples, the angle is never 90°.

Eigenvectors at a right angle

  • Now make the matrix symmetric: \(S = S^T\).
  • The two eigenvector lines are always 90° apart.
  • Perpendicular eigenvectors make the factoring that follows possible.
Act 4 / Spectral theorem

The spectral theorem

A real symmetric \(S\) has

  • real eigenvalues
  • orthonormal eigenvectors
$$S = Q\,\Lambda\,Q^{T}, \qquad Q^{-1} = Q^{T}$$

No inverse to compute: the inverse of \(Q\) is its transpose.

Orthogonal, diagonal, orthogonal

  • Any symmetric \(S\) splits into three matrices: \(S = Q\Lambda Q^T\).
  • \(Q\) and \(Q^T\) are orthogonal: rotations.
  • \(\Lambda\) is diagonal: a stretch along the axes.
  • A complicated map becomes three simple moves.

What goes into \(Q\) and \(\Lambda\)

  • Columns of \(Q\): the unit eigenvectors of \(S\).
  • Diagonal of \(\Lambda\): their eigenvalues, in the same order.
  • \(Q^T\): the columns of \(Q\) laid down as rows.
  • \(3\times3\): three eigenvectors, three eigenvalues.
  • The clip writes \(\vec e_i\) for our \(\mathbf{q}_i\).

\(\Lambda\) scales the axes

  • \(\Lambda\) is diagonal, so it acts on each axis alone.
  • \(\Lambda\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1 x\\ \lambda_2 y\end{bmatrix}\)
  • The \(x\)-axis stretches by \(\lambda_1\), the \(y\)-axis by \(\lambda_2\).

\(Q\) turns the axes onto the eigenvectors

  • \(Q\) is orthogonal, so it is a rotation.
  • Which rotation? The one that turns the standard basis onto the eigenvectors.

\(Q\mathbf{e}_1 = \mathbf{q}_1\), \(Q\mathbf{e}_2 = \mathbf{q}_2\)

  • A matrix times \((1,0)\) picks out its first column.
  • So \(Q\mathbf{e}_1 = \mathbf{q}_1\): the \(x\)-axis goes to the first eigenvector.
  • Likewise \(Q\mathbf{e}_2 = \mathbf{q}_2\).

Column \(i\) of \(Q\) is eigenvector \(i\)

  • \(Q\) turns the \(i\)-th standard basis vector onto the \(i\)-th eigenvector.
  • \(Q^{-1} = Q^T\) turns it back: the reverse rotation.
  • The clip writes \(Q^{-1}\); for an orthogonal \(Q\) that is \(Q^T\).

Choose \(Q\) to be a rotation

  • Flip the sign of an eigenvector and it is still an eigenvector.
  • Pick the signs so that \(\det Q = +1\).
  • Then \(Q\) is a pure rotation, with no reflection.

Our \(S\), factored

  • \(S = \begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}\)
  • \(\lambda_1 = 6\), \(\mathbf{q}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)\)
  • \(\lambda_2 = 2\), \(\mathbf{q}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(-1,1)\)
  • \(Q\) is a 45° rotation.

What \(S\) does in 2D

  • Apply \(S\) to the rectangle in one go.
  • It becomes a large parallelogram.
  • Next: the same result in three moves.

Rotate, scale, rotate back

  • \(Q^T\): rotate the eigenvectors onto the axes.
  • \(\Lambda\): scale \(x\) by 6 and \(y\) by 2.
  • \(Q\): rotate the axes back onto the eigenvectors.
  • The final shape is exactly what \(S\) gave.
Act 4 / Check

The same \(S\), numerically

\(\lambda_1 = 6\), \(\mathbf{q}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)\); \(\lambda_2 = 2\), \(\mathbf{q}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(-1,1)\). Columns in that order make \(Q\) a rotation.

$$S = \sum_i \lambda_i\,\mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^{T} = 6\cdot\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix} + 2\cdot\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}$$

A symmetric matrix is a sum of rank-one pieces, one per eigenvector.

A negative eigenvalue flips

  • \(S = \begin{bmatrix}-1&-2\\-2&-1.8\end{bmatrix}\): \(\lambda = 0.64\) and \(-3.44\).
  • \(Q^T\) rotates, as before.
  • \(\Lambda\) shrinks along \(\mathbf{q}_1\), and stretches and flips along \(\mathbf{q}_2\).
  • A negative eigenvalue reverses its direction.

A \(3\times3\) \(S\), factored

  • A \(3\times3\) symmetric \(S\).
  • \(Q\): three perpendicular unit eigenvectors.
  • \(\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(4.15,\,-1.56,\,1.31)\)

The same in 3D

  • \(S\) deforms the coloured cube in one go.
  • Now take it apart in three steps.

Step 1: rotate with \(Q^T\)

  • \(Q^T\) rotates the three perpendicular eigenvectors.
  • They land on the \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) axes.

Step 2: scale with \(\Lambda\)

  • Scale \(x\) by 4.15, \(y\) by \(-1.56\), \(z\) by 1.31.
  • The negative factor flips the \(y\) direction.
  • \(\Lambda\) does all three at once; the clip splits them up.

Step 3: rotate back with \(Q\)

  • \(Q\) rotates the axes back onto the eigenvectors.
  • The result matches what \(S\) did in one go.
Act 4 / Back to the bowl

Eigenvalues are the curvatures of \(x^TSx\)

In Block 2.2, the bowl \(z=x^TSx\) had two axes at right angles, with a steepness \(\lambda\) along each. Now we can name them.

Put \(S=Q\Lambda Q^T\) in, and write \(y=Q^Tx\), the coordinates of \(x\) along the eigenvectors:

$$x^TSx=x^TQ\Lambda Q^Tx=(Q^Tx)^T\Lambda(Q^Tx)=y^T\Lambda y=\lambda_1y_1^2+\lambda_2y_2^2$$

The axes of the bowl are the eigenvectors, and the steepness along each is its eigenvalue.

\(S\) is positive definite exactly when every eigenvalue is positive.

Act 4 / Inverse and powers

Inverse and powers from \(Q\Lambda Q^T\)

Let \(S = Q\Lambda Q^T\) with every \(\lambda_i \neq 0\). Two facts from Block 2.2: \(Q^{-1} = Q^T\), and \((BC)^{-1} = C^{-1}B^{-1}\).

$$S^{-1} = (Q\Lambda Q^T)^{-1} = (Q^T)^{-1}\Lambda^{-1}Q^{-1} = Q\Lambda^{-1}Q^T,\qquad \Lambda^{-1} = \mathrm{diag}(1/\lambda_1,\ 1/\lambda_2)$$

Powers: in a product of copies, each middle \(Q^TQ\) is \(I\) and cancels.

$$S^2 = Q\Lambda(Q^TQ)\Lambda Q^T = Q\Lambda^2Q^T,\qquad S^k = Q\Lambda^kQ^T,\qquad S^{-k} = (S^{-1})^k = Q\Lambda^{-k}Q^T$$

To raise \(S\) to a power, raise each eigenvalue to it. The eigenvectors stay the same.

Block 2.2 / Act 3 / Definition

Orthonormal columns

\(Q\) is orthogonal when its columns are unit vectors at right angles to each other.

$$Q^TQ = I \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Q^{-1}=Q^T$$

\(Q^{-1}\) is the matrix that undoes \(Q\).

Entry \((i,j)\) of \(Q^TQ\) is column \(i\) dotted with column \(j\): 1 on the diagonal, 0 elsewhere.

To undo an orthogonal matrix, transpose it. No elimination needed.

Act 4 / Worked problem

Problem 1

When \(A = S\Lambda S^{-1}\) is a real-symmetric (or Hermitian) matrix, eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormal and hence \(S = Q\) is orthogonal (or unitary). Thus, \(A = Q\Lambda Q^T\), which is called the spectral decomposition of \(A\).

Find the spectral decomposition for \(A = \begin{pmatrix}3&2\\2&3\end{pmatrix}\), and check by explicit multiplication that \(A = Q\Lambda Q^T\). Hence, find \(A^{-3}\) and \(\cos(A\pi/3)\).

Here \(S\) is the matrix of eigenvectors (our \(P\) in Act 5), not a symmetric matrix.

Act 4 / Worked problem

Solution: eigenvalues, eigenvectors, \(Q\Lambda Q^T\)

The characteristic equation for \(A\) is \(\lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 5 = 0\). Thus the eigenvalues of \(A\) are \(\lambda_1 = 1\) and \(\lambda_2 = 5\).

For \(\lambda_1 = 1\), the eigenvector is \(\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\). For \(\lambda_2 = 5\), the eigenvector is \(\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\).

Thus the spectral decomposition of \(A\) is

$$A = \begin{pmatrix}\sqrt2/2&\sqrt2/2\\-\sqrt2/2&\sqrt2/2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&5\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt2/2&-\sqrt2/2\\\sqrt2/2&\sqrt2/2\end{pmatrix}.$$
Act 4 / Worked problem

Check by explicit multiplication

We check the above decomposition by explicit multiplication:

$$\begin{aligned}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt2/2&\sqrt2/2\\-\sqrt2/2&\sqrt2/2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&5\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt2/2&-\sqrt2/2\\\sqrt2/2&\sqrt2/2\end{pmatrix} &= \begin{pmatrix}\sqrt2/2&5\sqrt2/2\\-\sqrt2/2&5\sqrt2/2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt2/2&-\sqrt2/2\\\sqrt2/2&\sqrt2/2\end{pmatrix}\\[4pt] &= \begin{pmatrix}3&2\\2&3\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}$$
Act 4 / Worked problem

\(A^{-3}\)

Now

$$\begin{aligned}A^{-3} &= \begin{pmatrix}\sqrt2/2&\sqrt2/2\\-\sqrt2/2&\sqrt2/2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&5^{-3}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt2/2&-\sqrt2/2\\\sqrt2/2&\sqrt2/2\end{pmatrix}\\[4pt] &= \begin{pmatrix}(1+5^{-3})/2&(-1+5^{-3})/2\\(-1+5^{-3})/2&(1+5^{-3})/2\end{pmatrix}\end{aligned}$$
Act 4 / Worked problem

\(\cos(A\pi/3)\)

Why: \(\cos x = 1 - \tfrac{x^2}{2!} + \tfrac{x^4}{4!} - \cdots\) uses only powers, and each power of \(A\) is \(Q\Lambda^kQ^T\). So \(\cos(A\pi/3) = Q\cos(\Lambda\pi/3)Q^T\): apply \(\cos\) to each eigenvalue.

and

$$\begin{aligned}\cos(A\pi/3) &= \begin{pmatrix}\sqrt2/2&\sqrt2/2\\-\sqrt2/2&\sqrt2/2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\pi/3)&0\\0&\cos(5\pi/3)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt2/2&-\sqrt2/2\\\sqrt2/2&\sqrt2/2\end{pmatrix}\\[4pt] &= \begin{pmatrix}1/2&0\\0&1/2\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}$$
